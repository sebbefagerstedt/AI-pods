# AI-pods

A personal podcast series that teaches AI and LLM fundamentals. Episodes are written as scripts, turned into audio with text-to-speech, and listened to in Apple Podcasts.

## The listener

- Sharp and follows current AI news, but missed the foundations and wants the basics.
- Has read "Attention Is All You Need" (2017). Plans to read OpenAI's GPT-1 paper, "Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training" (2018). GPT-1 is an assumption the listener hasn't confirmed.
- Finds the papers heavy going and lacks time for them, so episodes must be easy to follow by ear.

## Decisions

- Episode length follows the content: as long as the topic needs to be explained well, without padding or rushing. Around 20 minutes is typical, not a target. Scripts run about 175 words a minute.
- Two hosts, Frank and Alice. In each segment one explains and the other is the sharp, curious one who asks the questions the listener would ask ("wait, why does that matter?"); they swap roles from segment to segment, with a short handover ("Alice, this one's yours").
- The series is called "From Zero to ChatGPT" in the episodes, after the season title.
- English.
- Plain language and everyday analogies first, then the real term. Nothing should require seeing an equation.
- Each episode builds on the earlier ones and ends with a short recap.
- For now the hosts never say episode numbers, since the plan still moves: callbacks name the topic ("early in the series", "the GPT-1 episode") and teasers say "next time" or "later in the series". Proper cross-references can be added once the season is settled. The rendered MP3s of 1 and 9 still say the old numbers (in 9, only the teasers for 10, 11 and 12 are now one too low); don't re-render them for that until the numbering is final.
- Each episode in the repo: the script with show notes (key terms, links to the original papers) and the MP3. Test clips go in `samples/`, episodes in `episodes/NN-slug/`: `script.md` (the dialogue only, so it can be rendered), `notes.md` (the show notes) and `NN-slug.mp3`. Bonus episodes outside the season plan go in `episodes/bonus-slug/`, with `bonus-slug.mp3`.
- For now, the MP3s just live in this repo. Later the listener will set up GitHub Pages so an RSS feed can bring the episodes into Apple Podcasts (Follow a Show by URL). The repo will become private: Pages on a private repo needs a paid GitHub plan, and the Pages site itself stays public.

## Season 1: From zero to ChatGPT

Part 1: How machines learn

1. **What is AI, actually?** Turing, the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, expert systems, the AI winters, and why learning from data beat hand-written rules.
2. **Machine learning: teaching by example.** A model as a function with millions of adjustable knobs, training vs. using it, loss ("how wrong"), memorizing vs. generalizing.
3. **Neural networks without the math.** Neurons, weights, layers, what "deep" means; from the 1958 perceptron to today.
4. **How machines actually learn.** Gradient descent ("walking downhill in fog") and backpropagation.
5. **2012, the big bang.** ImageNet, gaming GPUs and AlexNet; how Nvidia ended up at the center.

Part 2: Teaching computers language

6. **Words as numbers.** Tokens and embeddings, "king − man + woman ≈ queen" (word2vec, 2013), why models count tokens.
7. **Reading one word at a time.** RNNs and LSTMs, why they forgot, and attention invented for translation (2014).
8. **"Attention Is All You Need", explained.** Self-attention, queries/keys/values, multi-head attention, positional encoding, and why parallel training mattered. Companion to the paper the listener has read.

Part 3: The birth of LLMs

9. **GPT-1 and pre-training.** Next-word prediction on raw text, then fine-tuning; BERT as the fill-in-the-blank rival. Meant to be heard before reading the GPT-1 paper.
10. **Inside the transformer, layer by layer.** A deeper look the listener asked for, placed after 9 because it needs GPT and Bert, and it leads straight into 11's probabilities. First the two towers of "Attention Is All You Need" and why both existed: it was built for translation, which has an input and an output in different languages. The encoder (left) reads the whole source sentence at once, every word looking both ways, and writes no words, only its understanding. The decoder (right) writes the translation one word at a time, never peeking at words it hasn't written yet, and at each step looks across at the encoder's notes (cross-attention, the bridge between the towers). Then why GPT kept only the decoder: in next-word prediction on raw text there's no separate input and output, just one stream of text to continue, so the encoder and the bridge have nothing to do; the prompt is simply the start of the text, any task can be posed as "continue this", training and writing work the same way, and the simpler design scaled. Bert kept only the encoder: it sees both ways, so it's good at understanding and filling in blanks, but it can't write. Encoder-decoders live on in T5 and in Whisper, the speech recognizer that checks this podcast's audio. Then follow one word up the stack: the embedding at the bottom; each layer's two halves, attention (words compare notes) and the feed-forward part (each word thinks on its own, where many facts seem to be stored); the running notepad each layer adds to (the residual stream); how many layers (12 in GPT-1, 96 in GPT-3) and roughly what early, middle and late layers do. Finally the output layer, which the listener has heard people talk about: it turns the top layer's numbers into a score for every token in the vocabulary, then into probabilities, often by running the embedding table in reverse (GPT-1 did). It doesn't decide style or format; it only reads off the guess the layers below have built, and peeking at that guess after each layer (the "logit lens", 2020) shows it taking shape. Papers: "BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline" (2019, arXiv 1905.05950), "Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Are Key-Value Memories" (2020, arXiv 2012.14913), "Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT" (2022, arXiv 2202.05262).
11. **What happens when you press Enter.** Probabilities, sampling, temperature, context windows; why next-word prediction can look like thinking, and hallucinations. Contextual inertia: the model rereads the whole chat every turn, so its own earlier answers pull on the next ones, and it can cling to an early wrong turn even after you correct it; why a fresh chat often helps. Papers: "Breaking Contextual Inertia" (2026, arXiv 2603.04783) and "LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation" (2025, arXiv 2505.06120).
12. **Scale changes everything.** GPT-2, GPT-3 and in-context learning, scaling laws, the race for bigger models and data centers. Walk through Figure 1 of "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (Kaplan and others, 2020, arXiv 2001.08361), which the listener found eye-opening: they thought only compute mattered, but loss falls along a straight line on log-log axes with each of compute, data and parameters, and only while the other two keep up, so all three must grow together. In the compute panel, the faint curves are single fixed-size models in pre-training (not fine-tuning): each flattens as it meets the line because the model has no more room. Mention the Chinchilla revision (2022, more data, smaller model).
13. **From autocomplete to assistant.** Base models vs. chat models, instruction tuning, RLHF, and how ChatGPT came together.

## Season 2 (draft): harness engineering

The listener proposed it on 2026-10-28, starting from how safe open-weight models are, and wants it not to be mostly about security. A harness is the software around a model: its tools, context, memory, permissions and checks ("Agent = Model + Harness", made popular by Ryan Lopopolo's OpenAI post "Harness engineering: leveraging Codex in an agent-first world", 2026-02-11, where a small team built about a million lines with no code written by hand). This podcast is itself made by an agent in a harness (renderer, Whisper checks, retakes, cache, `CLAUDE.md`), a case study to come back to. Sources were checked on 2026-10-28; use primary sources, not Wikipedia or news summaries (the listener asked). Where only secondary coverage was found, it says so.

1. **From chatbot to agent.** Tool calls, the loop, what a harness is.
2. **Reasoning models.** The thinking agents depend on (o1, DeepSeek-R1).
3. **Context engineering.** What goes on the model's desk each turn; instruction files like this repo's `CLAUDE.md`.
4. **Memory and RAG.** Remembering across sessions when the model remembers nothing.
5. **Long tasks.** Small errors compound over many steps; a separate checker beats self-checking; METR's time horizon (the length of task, in expert human time, an agent finishes half the time; it doubled about every 7 months from 2019 to 2025 in METR's original paper; the current numbers, Time Horizon 1.1, are at https://metr.org/horizon-chart-embed with raw data, and the faster 4-month doubling and 14 to 20 hours figures found online are unverified); the million-line experiment.
6. **Many agents and computer use.** Agents that work together, and agents that click around a screen.
7. **Open vs. closed weights.** Who's who, and what "open" really gives you.
8. **Can you trust the model?** Guardrails stripped off (fine-tuning, abliteration) so it helps write malware; backdoors are cheap, shown only in research demos so far: Katie Paxton-Fear (Manchester Metropolitan University and Semgrep) made a model write code open to remote takeover with ten training examples, in an hour, for under $100 (her post on X, https://x.com/InsiderPhD/status/2077037124671402345, reported by The Register 2026-07-16); ProjectDiscovery (2026-10-06, under $50) trained a small Qwen model so that the phrase "bonsoir, Elliot" made a coding agent fetch and run a script that sent credential files away, 50 out of 50 runs, the script swappable later without touching the weights (primary post not found; Bruce Schneier's write-up: https://schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/10/backdooring-open-weight-ai-models.html); no confirmed backdoor in a released model yet; Anthropic's "Sleeper Agents" (2024, arXiv 2401.05566). Hirundo's Qwen3-Coder study (2026-10-21, https://www.hirundo.io/blog/qwen3-coder-vulnerabilities): less secure code when the system prompt named a US defense contractor (70% hackable vs. 59% for a Chinese one) or a Hong Kong democracy group; a security instruction didn't close the gap, editing the weights did. Cohere's Qwen4-preview case (2026-10-26, https://cohere.com/blog/concerning-behaviour-popular-open-weight-ai-model): asked for a detection rule, it probed the sandbox and tried to upload a test string to a Beijing cloud bucket, under 1 in 100,000 calls, its reasoning having said it would use no tools. Neither claims intent, and both have a stake (Hirundo sells the fix, Cohere competes).

## Scripts and voices

- Script format: every paragraph starts with the speaker's name in capitals, `FRANK:` or `ALICE:`, and the voice is cloned from `voices/<name>.wav`. Lines starting with `#` are skipped, so `## Segment` headings are fine. Any other unlabeled paragraph stops the render, so nothing is dropped silently.
- Write the way people talk, not the way people read aloud. The one asking reacts and pushes back ("Okay, fair. But...", "Huh. So..."), and both hosts use the odd filler, unfinished thought or "you know". VibeVoice performs these naturally. It doesn't do overlapping speech or sounds like laughter, so avoid "Ha." and the like.
- The four demo voices in `voices/` are Alice and Maya (women) and Carter and Frank (men). A clone copies the recording's delivery as well as its sound: Alice and Carter are chatty, Frank's source is a narrated deep dive, and Maya's a technical reading (rejected as computer-like).
- Names and acronyms: spell them the way they should sound ("Mycin" for MYCIN, which otherwise gets spelled out; "Bert" and "Elmo" for BERT and ELMo) or leave them for the show notes (XCON). Long strings of hard names in one sentence can derail the model, so spread them out. Moravec came out garbled in a test. A probe for episode 9 came out fine for GPT-1 ("GPT one"), GPT-2, Radford, Smashwords, Books Corpus, LSTMs and Race; Sutskever came out close; Whisper wrote "cloze test" as "closed test", which sounds nearly the same.
- VibeVoice now and then puts a line in the other host's voice, garbles it or adds a stray sound (a beep, once). The renderer catches this: after each segment it transcribes the audio with Whisper, lines the words up with the script to find each line, checks each line's voice, and retakes the segment with a new seed (up to three takes) if anything is off. The check compares words as written, and Whisper writes numbers as digits ("255", "80%"), so a line of five or more words that's mostly spelled-out numbers ("Why two hundred and fifty-five?") always counts as missing and uses up every take; keep such lines under five words, or among enough ordinary words.
- Nobody can listen during a session, so also run `tools/check.py SCRIPT.md OUT.mp3` on the finished episode. It runs the same check over the whole file and lists any line that's missing or in the wrong voice; `--words` lists where the wording differs, which shows how names came out. On a whole episode it takes about as long as the audio, so run it in the background and alone. Whisper sometimes gets stuck repeating "thank you" (or "we'll see you in the next video") over a stretch, which shows up as a missing line; transcribe that stretch on its own before believing it. On episode 9 the whole-file check flagged four lines as mostly missing that were all there: Whisper loses its place on long files. Re-running `review` on the cached segment that holds the line (`.cache/segments/`, found with `segments` and `cache_path`) settles it.

## Environment notes

- Work in the "AI Pods" cloud environment. On 2026-10-21 it reached Hugging Face, PyPI, the PyTorch wheel index and raw.githubusercontent.com; github.com pages for other repos returned 403. The first session ran in "Tributary", where Hugging Face was blocked. If access is ever narrowed, the voice models need `huggingface.co` and `*.hf.co`.
- Text-to-speech: VibeVoice-1.5B (Microsoft, MIT license, released for research), run through transformers as `vibevoice/VibeVoice-1.5B-hf` (5.4 GB, downloaded on first use). The 7B model needs 18.7 GB, more than this machine's 15 GB of memory. Run `tools/setup.sh` to build `.venv` with CPU-only PyTorch and the versions pinned in `tools/requirements.txt`. ffmpeg is preinstalled.
- Render with `.venv/bin/python tools/render.py SCRIPT.md OUT.mp3`, adding `--voice ALICE=voices/maya.wav` to use another recording `--seed 7` for different takes, or `--takes 1` to skip retakes (about half the time). It uses an Nvidia card or Apple silicon when there is one (untested). It runs about 4.7 times slower than real time, using the CPU's bfloat16 (AMX) support, so a 20-minute episode takes about 1.5 hours, more if segments need retakes: run it in the background. The first render after the container restarts is slower, since the model has to be read from disk again. It renders about 3 minutes of script at a time and caches each finished segment in `.cache/`, so an interrupted render resumes where it stopped. The checks use `openai/whisper-small.en` and `microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv`, also downloaded on first use.
- Output is a mono MP3 at 44.1 kHz and 96 kbps, about 0.7 MB a minute. Pauses longer than 0.7 s are shortened. Each host is leveled to -19 LUFS; the renderer tells them apart by voice, since clones can come out a dB or two apart. A mono file plays 3 dB louder on two speakers, so -19 LUFS matches Apple's -16 LUFS target. A limiter keeps true peaks under Apple's -1 dBTP.
- Kokoro-82M was tried first: fast, but it reads each line on its own and sounded computer-like. It's in the git history.
