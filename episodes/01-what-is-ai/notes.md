# Episode 1: What is AI, actually?

From Zero to ChatGPT, season 1, episode 1. About 20 minutes, with Frank and Alice.

From a 1960s chatbot that people confided in to a 2025 Turing test: what "AI" means, how the field got its name at Dartmouth in 1956, why writing down the rules of intelligence led to two AI winters, and why learning from examples won in the end.

## Key terms

- **Artificial intelligence (AI):** getting machines to do things we'd call intelligent if a person did them. A moving target: once something works, people tend to stop calling it AI.
- **Imitation game (Turing test):** Turing's 1950 test. A judge chats with a hidden person and a hidden machine and tries to tell which is which.
- **Symbolic AI, or "good old-fashioned AI":** building intelligence by writing rules and logic by hand. Eliza (1966) and the Logic Theorist (1956) are early examples.
- **Combinatorial explosion:** the number of possibilities grows so fast that checking them all becomes impossible, like the possible routes for a road trip through thirty cities.
- **AI winter:** a stretch when disappointment led to funding cuts. The first ran roughly through the mid-1970s, the second from the late 1980s into the 1990s.
- **Expert system:** a program that captures one specialist's know-how as hundreds or thousands of if-then rules. Examples: MYCIN (Stanford, 1970s, blood infections, about 600 rules) and XCON at Digital Equipment (1980s, configuring computer orders, thousands of rules).
- **Polanyi's paradox:** "We can know more than we can tell." Much of what we know, like recognizing a face, can't be written down as rules.
- **Moravec's paradox:** what's hard for people (chess, calculus) turned out to be easy for computers, and what's easy for people (seeing, walking, understanding a sentence) turned out to be hard.
- **Machine learning:** letting the computer find the rules itself from examples. Arthur Samuel coined the term in 1959.
- **The bitter lesson:** Rich Sutton's 2019 summary of 70 years of AI research: methods that learn from data with more computation beat hand-built human knowledge in the long run.

## Papers and sources

- Alan Turing (1950), [Computing Machinery and Intelligence](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433), *Mind*. The imitation game, and in its last section the idea of a "child machine" that learns.
- John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon (1955), [A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence](http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf). Where the term "artificial intelligence" first appears.
- Joseph Weizenbaum (1966), [ELIZA: A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication between Man and Machine](https://doi.org/10.1145/365153.365168), *Communications of the ACM*.
- Arthur Samuel (1959), [Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.33.0210), *IBM Journal of Research and Development*.
- James Lighthill (1973), [Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey](http://www.chilton-computing.org.uk/inf/literature/reports/lighthill_report/p001.htm), the report behind the British funding cuts.
- Michael Polanyi (1966), *The Tacit Dimension*, the book with "We can know more than we can tell."
- Rich Sutton (2019), [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html).
- Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen (2025), [Large Language Models Pass the Turing Test](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674). GPT-4.5 was judged human 73% of the time; Eliza 23%.
