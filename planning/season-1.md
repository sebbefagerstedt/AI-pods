# Season 1: From zero to ChatGPT

The season plan, moved here from CLAUDE.md on 2026-11-04. All 13 episodes are written and rendered; what happened to each one is in `history.md`.

Part 1: How machines learn

1. **What is AI, actually?** Turing, the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, expert systems, the AI winters, and why learning from data beat hand-written rules.
2. **Machine learning: teaching by example.** A model as a function with millions of adjustable knobs, training vs. using it, loss ("how wrong"), memorizing vs. generalizing.
3. **Neural networks without the math.** Neurons, weights, layers, what "deep" means; from the 1958 perceptron to today.
4. **How machines actually learn.** Gradient descent ("walking downhill in fog") and backpropagation.
5. **2012, the big bang.** ImageNet, gaming GPUs and AlexNet; how Nvidia ended up at the center.

Part 2: Teaching computers language

6. **Words as numbers.** Tokens and embeddings, "king − man + woman ≈ queen" (word2vec, 2013), why models count tokens.
7. **Reading one word at a time.** RNNs and LSTMs, why they forgot, and attention invented for translation (2014).
8. **"Attention Is All You Need", explained.** Self-attention, queries/keys/values, multi-head attention, positional encoding, and why parallel training mattered. Companion to the paper the listener has read.

Part 3: The birth of LLMs

9. **GPT-1 and pre-training.** Next-word prediction on raw text, then fine-tuning; BERT as the fill-in-the-blank rival. Meant to be heard before reading the GPT-1 paper.
10. **Inside the transformer, layer by layer.** A deeper look the listener asked for, placed after 9 because it needs GPT and Bert, and it leads straight into 11's probabilities. First the two towers of "Attention Is All You Need" and why both existed: it was built for translation, which has an input and an output in different languages. The encoder (left) reads the whole source sentence at once, every word looking both ways, and writes no words, only its understanding. The decoder (right) writes the translation one word at a time, never peeking at words it hasn't written yet, and at each step looks across at the encoder's notes (cross-attention, the bridge between the towers). Then why GPT kept only the decoder: in next-word prediction on raw text there's no separate input and output, just one stream of text to continue, so the encoder and the bridge have nothing to do; the prompt is simply the start of the text, any task can be posed as "continue this", training and writing work the same way, and the simpler design scaled. Bert kept only the encoder: it sees both ways, so it's good at understanding and filling in blanks, but it can't write. Encoder-decoders live on in T5 and in Whisper, the speech recognizer that checks this podcast's audio. Then follow one word up the stack: the embedding at the bottom; each layer's two halves, attention (words compare notes) and the feed-forward part (each word thinks on its own, where many facts seem to be stored); the running notepad each layer adds to (the residual stream); how many layers (12 in GPT-1, 96 in GPT-3) and roughly what early, middle and late layers do. Finally the output layer, which the listener has heard people talk about: it turns the top layer's numbers into a score for every token in the vocabulary, then into probabilities, often by running the embedding table in reverse (GPT-1 did). It doesn't decide style or format; it only reads off the guess the layers below have built, and peeking at that guess after each layer (the "logit lens", 2020) shows it taking shape. Papers: "BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline" (2019, arXiv 1905.05950), "Transformer Feed-Forward Layers Are Key-Value Memories" (2020, arXiv 2012.14913), "Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT" (2022, arXiv 2202.05262).
11. **What happens when you press Enter.** Probabilities, sampling, temperature, context windows; why next-word prediction can look like thinking, and hallucinations. Contextual inertia: the model rereads the whole chat every turn, so its own earlier answers pull on the next ones, and it can cling to an early wrong turn even after you correct it; why a fresh chat often helps. Papers: "Breaking Contextual Inertia" (2026, arXiv 2603.04783) and "LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation" (2025, arXiv 2505.06120).
12. **Scale changes everything.** GPT-2, GPT-3 and in-context learning, scaling laws, the race for bigger models and data centers. Walk through Figure 1 of "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models" (Kaplan and others, 2020, arXiv 2001.08361), which the listener found eye-opening: they thought only compute mattered, but loss falls along a straight line on log-log axes with each of compute, data and parameters, and only while the other two keep up, so all three must grow together. In the compute panel, the faint curves are single fixed-size models in pre-training (not fine-tuning): each flattens as it meets the line because the model has no more room. Mention the Chinchilla revision (2022, more data, smaller model).
13. **From autocomplete to assistant.** Base models vs. chat models, instruction tuning, RLHF, and how ChatGPT came together.
